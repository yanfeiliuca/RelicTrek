Windrose's Ten-Bell Budget: Planning a Fast Travel Network That Actually Pays Off

The first time you get two Fast Travel Points talking to each other in Windrose, the map shrinks. The second time you realize you only get ten of them, the map starts to feel like a puzzle. That shift — from "I can teleport now" to "where should my ten slots actually go?" — is where a lot of captains quietly lose hours. This piece isn't about finding your first bell. It's about what to do once bells stop being rare and start being a budget.

Bells Are a Copper Budget, Not a Collectible

Once you've smelted your first Copper Ingot, the Fast Travel Bell recipe opens up at a Level 1 Workbench: 10 Copper Ingots and 3 Rope per bell. Rope comes from plant fiber, so the real constraint is copper — and that copper competes with everything else you want to build early. Each Fast Travel Point then consumes one bell plus a stack of wood when you place it through the Build menu (most sources list 20 Wood; at least one lists 10, so check your own build menu after the latest patch).

The practical takeaway is to treat copper like a savings account. Players who bank a little copper on every mining trip end up with a spare bell ready when they reach somewhere worth anchoring, instead of sailing home to smelt.

Tip

Keep one crafted bell and enough wood for a point in your pack whenever you head into unexplored water. Dropping a point the moment you find a good spot is far cheaper than making the trip twice.

Spend Slots Where You'll Return, Not Where You've Been

You can run up to ten active Fast Travel Points, and the network won't work at all until at least two exist. The tempting move is to drop a point at every interesting beach. Resist it. A point earns its slot by how often you'll come back, not by how far away it is.

A sensible order: your main base first, since that's where you'll want to return from almost anywhere. Then the resource nodes you visit on a loop — copper and iron deposits especially, since they feed more bells. After that, dungeon entrances and quest hubs you know you'll revisit. One-off curiosities can wait.

The good news is that mistakes are cheap. Dismantling a point refunds its materials and frees the slot, so an early placement that stops being useful can be recycled once the next region opens.

The Free Points You Shouldn't Duplicate

Some faction locations — Tortuga among them — come with pre-placed points that activate when you first visit and don't count against your ten. That changes the math more than it sounds. If a faction hub already sits near the area you were going to anchor, you've just been handed a slot back. Visit those hubs early, then plan your own points around the gaps they leave.

Placement note

Points are placed by hand in the world — you can't drop one remotely from the map — and community reports say they can't sit right next to trading posts. Scout the spot before you commit the bell.

Warping With Your Ship (and Without It)

Here's the detail that catches people out: how you start the jump decides what comes with you. Warping while actively at the helm moves the ship and its crew. Opening the map and warping from the deck reportedly moves only your character, leaving the ship — and any co-op partners standing on it — behind. Helm warps also aren't available mid-combat or while you're being chased, so a point is an exit you plan for, not a panic button.

If you're playing with friends, community guides note that points are shared across the server, which makes splitting the work natural: one person banks copper, another scouts placements, and the whole crew benefits from a single network.

For true emergencies, the Homeward Journey potion remains a separate route back to your revival point. Set a respawn point before long voyages either way.

Why This Is Worth Your Time

Ten slots sounds generous until you're thirty hours in and every island has a reason to come back. Thinking of bells as a copper budget, letting faction hubs cover the ground they already cover, and warping from the helm when you want the ship along turns fast travel from a convenience into a real strategy. A well-planned network doesn't just save walking — it decides which parts of the world you can afford to keep exploring.
