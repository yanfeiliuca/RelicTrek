The Sea Floor Nobody Visits: Skellige's Underwater Treasure Layer

There's a particular kind of discovery that Witcher 3 players keep making years after finishing the game — not a new quest or a hidden character, but the realization that an entire zone was right there on the map the whole time, disguised as empty sea. Skellige's underwater layer is one of the most consistently overlooked regions in the game, and for one very understandable reason: the ocean looks like a gap between the real content, not the content itself.

That framing is wrong. The waters around Skellige's islands contain sunken ships, partially submerged cave systems, and a density of Relic-quality equipment that rivals any land zone in the game. If you completed Skellige and stayed mostly dry, you left the best loot behind.

Why Players Skip It

The map design is the culprit. Skellige's island map emphasizes land-based question marks, and the visual language of sea travel — sailing between islands, managing Siren encounters, watching the breath meter — trains players to think of water as transit space. When you've already dealt with Drowners and Sea Hags on land, the idea of swimming toward more of them, with a ticking oxygen timer, feels like optional punishment rather than reward.

The question marks in open water look identical to the ones on land. Nothing in the UI tells you that the one sitting 30 meters offshore from Ard Skellig contains a Relic silver sword in better condition than most of what you'll find through story content. The game trusts you to be curious enough to check.

The Four Zones Worth Your Time

Not all of Skellige's waters are equally rewarding. The northern and western coasts of Ard Skellig — approached by diving at the base of the cliffs — hold the densest cluster of useful material. Underground ruins sit at 5 to 15 meters depth, sheltering Drowner camps that guard chests containing Superior and Enhanced Witcher gear diagram components. This is the highest-priority zone for any player still building toward a full Witcher gear set.

Hindarsfjall's sea caves are in a different category entirely. Several cave entrances on this island are only reachable from the water — the map shows nothing useful here from a land perspective. Inside these partially submerged chambers, you'll encounter Water Hag variants and chests carrying Mastercrafted material components. One cave, adorned with ice formations, hides a diagram for Enhanced Ursine Trousers in an ornate chest that the game offers no obvious reason to look for. The "Hidden in the Depths" treasure hunt — triggered by a crumpled letter found near Harviken's shore — threads through exactly this kind of sea cave, making it an efficient anchor for a broader underwater sweep of the island.

An Skellig's southeastern waters contain several ship hulls with distinct profiles. Drowner corpses on these wrecks drop higher-tier loot than their land-based equivalents, and the wrecks themselves often have chests with Relic-quality silver swords. The Faroe channel sea-floor question marks round out the route — navigable by boat and dive, less dramatic than the cave systems but still worthwhile for the material haul.

Managing the Hazards

Sirens are the primary surface threat and the one that bites players who don't prepare. They attack boats, and if you dive leaving them alive, your boat may be gone when you come back up. The crossbow handles Sirens efficiently — two bolts is usually enough, and the weapon auto-targets aerial enemies without requiring careful aiming. Clear the air before you dive.

Water Hags in the enclosed sea caves are the more dangerous encounter. Their underground surfacing attack is fast and hard to read in cramped spaces. Activate Quen before entering any cave entrance to give yourself a buffer for the first hit. Inside the cave you'll have room to fight properly, but that first surprise hit is the one that tends to kill mid-level Geralts.

The breath meter is more of a pacing constraint than a real danger, but it shapes how you plan dives. The Killer Whale potion — craftable from Hellebore Petals, Buckthorn, and Cuttlefish Bone among other ingredients — extends the breath meter significantly and makes complex wreck dives far more manageable. The Cat potion is also worth having: visibility underwater can be poor, and the Cat's night vision dramatically improves your ability to spot chests on the sea floor before you've exhausted your air.

The Practical Route

The full sweep takes around four hours and is best approached in phases. Start at Kaer Trolde harbor on Ard Skellig and work counterclockwise around the main island, clearing every coastal and sea question mark before moving on. This front-loads the zone with the best diagram density. Then cross to the smaller islands — Hindarsfjall first for the cave systems, then An Skellig for the shipwrecks, then Faroe for the channel floor. The open-water wrecks in the sea between islands are the final phase, requiring dedicated sailing trips to each location.

You won't need the loot from every individual chest. What you'll come away with is a near-complete set of Witcher gear diagram components, several Relic weapons that exceed anything available from land quests at equivalent story progression, and a genuine sense of having explored a zone most Skellige veterans left blank.

Worth the Dive

Skellige rewards the player who treats the sea as terrain rather than scenery. The underwater layer doesn't announce itself or offer waypoints — it just sits there, dense with equipment and quiet, waiting for someone to swim toward the question marks instead of sailing past them. Four hours, a couple of potions, and a crossbow with enough bolts. That's the entire ask. The return is some of the best loot in the game and the particular satisfaction of finishing a zone the game assumed you'd skip.
